# A. RESEARCH BUDGETS

These are **research benchmarks**. They are expenditure budgets built by groups of members of the public. They are not observed spending, not incomes, and not a poverty line.

## A1. Minimum Income Standard 2026

**Confirmed:** a 2026 report exists, published 8 September 2026 (S1). It supersedes MIS 2025.

**Major change that breaks the brief's assumption:** MIS 2026 no longer assumes social rent for pensioners. S1: "For the first time, MIS budgets for all household types now assume accommodation in the private rented sector."

## A3. Statements about changes of basket or assumptions between editions

Check status for all: **FETCHED-AND-QUOTED**.

### A3-01 `mis-2026-tenure-change`

S1: "Since 2008, groups have agreed that social rented accommodation would meet the needs of pensioners and households with children." In 2026 this changed to private renting. Effect stated by S1: "moving from social to private renting led to a 50.1% increase in rent for households with children, and a 34.3% increase for pensioner couples." Total budget including rent rose 12.3% for pensioner couples, "principally because of this change in tenure".

S1a Appendix Table 1 note: "Working-age adults in private rental sector (PRS), households with children and pensioners in social housing 2008-2025, PRS in 2026." Any time series of "% of MIS covered" crosses this break.

### A3-02 `mis-2026-basket-changes-pensioners`

- Healthcare: "groups in the review included an additional annual healthcare budget of £200 for individuals of pension age".
- Hairdressing budgets raised. Together these raised personal goods and services by "£13.11 each week, or 21.4%" for the pensioner couple.
- Takeaway budget raised; food and drink up £5.12 a week for the couple.
- Fuel method updated: "the assumptions informing the calculation of domestic fuel costs within MIS have been updated this year". Fuel fell £2.80 a week (8.5%) for the couple.
- Household goods fell because "in private rented housing the pensioner couple would not need to meet the cost of flooring".
- S1: "in 2026 changes in the basket of goods and services ... have generally had a greater impact on what has happened to budgets overall than inflation."
- S1 note: "Some numbers for 2025 in this section have changed slightly since the last report due to a correction in the deduction of council tax from incomes."

### A3-03 `rls-edition-history`

S2: budgets were uprated by CPI in 2020, 2022 and 2024; reviewed in 2021; and "In 2023, the RLS were 'rebased' - starting from scratch with groups of members of the public". The 2025 update is an inflation uprating only. S2 Table 1 shows the effect of the rebase: single moderate rose 34.3% from 2022 to 2023, and single minimum fell 6.8% from 2023 to 2024. Year-on-year changes therefore mix price change with basket change.

S2 also states that "the review being undertaken in 2026 is the first new research with members of the public since 2023". A further edition with changed baskets and housing assumptions should be expected.

### A3-04 `rls-london-minimum-housing-change`

S2: London groups agreed "for the first time, that the budget for a minimum living standard should include the cost of private rather than social rents for retired households." The headline London minimum figure (A2-01) still excludes rent.

# Definitional traps

| Ref | Trap |
|---|---|
| T1 | **MIS 2026 changed tenure.** Pensioner budgets now assume private rent, not social rent. Totals including rent are not comparable with any earlier MIS year. Use "total excluding rent" for comparisons, and even that moved because of basket changes. |
| T2 | **The RLS published in 2026 is priced at April 2025.** MIS 2026 is priced at April 2026. They are one year apart. |
| T3 | **RLS "minimum" is the MIS 2025 pensioner budget.** Single £266.62 and couple £430.66 a week appear in both. RLS minimum and MIS 2026 must not be shown as two independent benchmarks agreeing or disagreeing. |
| T4 | **RLS headline figures exclude rent and mortgage, but include council tax, water and fuel.** They are spending needed, so income must be higher once tax is paid. |
| T5 | **MIS has several totals.** Excluding rent (£273.57 single, £451.91 couple), benefits comparison basis (£250.01, £420.49), AHC-comparable (£237.43, £405.65), BHC-comparable (£386.94, £581.59). The "90% of MIS" statement uses the benefits comparison basis. |
| T6 | **Family Spending category 4 is not total housing cost.** Council tax and mortgage interest are in category 13. Health and Education are suppressed for retired households. |
| T7 | **Three different definitions of "retired".** Family Spending Table A23 (retired household, by dependence on state pensions), Table A17 (household reference person retired), and HCI (retired members supply at least half of gross household income). |
| T8 | **Pensioner unit, household, individual.** Pensioners' Incomes uses pensioner units and unequivalised income. HBAI counts individuals by equivalised household income. Family Spending and tenure use households. DWP State Pension statistics count individuals. |
| T9 | **Pensioners' Incomes net income is already net of council tax.** MIS and RLS budgets include council tax as a cost. Comparing the two without adjustment counts council tax twice. |
| T10 | **State Pension £248 (median per pensioner unit, FYE 2025, survey) is not State Pension £211.31 (mean per person, March 2026, administrative).** Different unit, average, date and source. |
| T11 | **HBAI relative and absolute low income are identical in FYE 2025** because the reference year was reset. |
